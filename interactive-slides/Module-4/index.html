The Credit Market

Just like products, money has a market driven by Supply and Demand.

🤔 Who are the players?

Think about who needs cash and who has cash.

  • Demand (Borrowers): Companies needing cash for Operations, Investing, or Refinancing.
  • Supply (Lenders): Trade Creditors, Banks, Public Debt Investors.

Demand: Operating Activities

Why do companies need cash for daily ops?

  • Cyclical Needs: Making goods before selling them.
  • Seasonality: Building inventory for holidays.
  • Operating Losses: Covering cash burn during slumps.

Thought Bubble

Loss-funding loans aren’t automatically “high risk.” If losses are temporary and explainable and the borrower has a credible path back to cash generation, the loan may function as a short-term bridge rather than distress financing.

Supply: Trade Credit

What It Is

Suppliers deliver goods now and allow payment later. It is routine, typically non-interest bearing, and acts as built-in short-term funding.

  • Net 30: Pay full amount in 30 days.
  • 2/10 Net 30: Pay within 10 days to take a 2% discount; otherwise pay in full by day 30.

"The First Line of Defense"

If a firm is under cash pressure, stretching payables (paying later) is often the first action taken before missing bank or bond payments.

The First Domino

Deep Dive: Term Loans

Key Mechanics

Typically used for assets (Machines, Factories).

Interest: Floating (SOFR + Spread)
Amortization: Principal paid over time

Definition: SOFR

Secured Overnight Financing Rate. The cost of borrowing cash overnight backed by Treasury securities. It replaced LIBOR as the global benchmark.

The "Spread"

The bank charges a base rate (SOFR) plus a "Risk Premium" (Spread).

Risky Client = Higher Spread.

Check Your Understanding: If SOFR is 4% and the bank charges a 2% spread, what is your interest rate?

History: The LIBOR Scandal

What was LIBOR?

The London Interbank Offered Rate was the global benchmark for 30 years. It represented the cost for banks to borrow from each other unsecured.

How it was set: A panel of banks submitted estimates of their borrowing costs. The top and bottom outliers were trimmed, and the rest averaged.

Why it failed

In times of stress, there were no actual transactions. Submissions were based on "judgment," leaving the door open for massive fraud.

The outcome: Huge fines ($9B+) and a forced transition to SOFR.

Deep Dive: Mortgages

Secured by real estate property.

  • Terms: Long (10-30 years).
  • Security: The lender has a "Lien" on the building.

Definition: Lien

A legal right to seize the property if the borrower fails to pay. The "Lien Holder" gets paid first from the sale proceeds.

Why use a Mortgage?

Because it's Secured, the interest rate is usually lower than other loans. The bank takes less risk because they can always take the building.

Lease Financing

Rent vs. Buy? In credit analysis, it's often treated the same.

Concrete Example: Starbucks

Starbucks doesn't usually buy the buildings for its coffee shops. It rents them for 10-20 years.

  • Credit Analyst View: "You signed a 20-year contract. You owe that money just like a loan."

Public Debt Markets

When companies outgrow bank limits or need cheaper long-term capital, they turn to the public markets.

Commercial Paper

(Short Term)

Commercial paper is short-term, low-cost, quickly issued funding for working capital—used instead of bonds when the need is temporary (but it requires strong credit and a backup liquidity line).

Bonds

(Long Term)

  • Traded on exchanges.
  • Debenture: Unsecured.
  • Bond: Usually Secured.

Private Credit Overview

Non-bank lending provided by private investment firms directly to companies.

What it is

A company needs financing (often fast, flexible, or complex). Instead of a bank or public bond, they borrow from a Private Credit Fund.

Common Types:

  • Direct Lending: Senior secured loans to mid-sized firms.
  • Mezzanine: Higher yield, lower priority (Junior debt).
  • Special Situations: Rescue financing for stressed firms.

Why use it?

  • Borrower Pros: Speed, certainty, and flexible terms that regulated banks can't offer.
  • Investor Pros: Higher yields than public bonds. Often secured.

Key Risks

Illiquidity: These loans are not traded on an exchange. Hard to sell quickly.

Opacity: Less public financial disclosure than bond issuers.

The Risk Analysis Process

How do we quantify the risk?

The 4-Step Process:

1. Purpose

Use of Proceeds: Is the cash for Growth (M&A, Capex) or Distress (covering losses)?

2. Macro

Industry & Economy: GDP trends, Regulatory changes, and Porter's 5 Forces.

3. Financials

Ratio Analysis: Historical Solvency, Liquidity, and Coverage metrics.

4. Future

3-Statement Model: Pro-forma projections to test repayment capacity.

Solvency Metrics: Measuring Leverage

Before we panic, we must choose the right measuring stick. What is in the Numerator?

1. Liabilities-to-Equity

Total LiabilitiesStockholders' Equity

Includes: Everything. Suppliers (A/P), Taxes, Wages, AND Debt.

Use When: Evaluating the total funding mix of the balance sheet. High ratio = Highly creditor funded.

2. Total Debt-to-Equity

Short Term Debt + Long Term DebtStockholders' Equity

Includes: Only interest-bearing financing (Loans, Bonds, Leases).

Use When: Focusing on financing risk, covenants, and interest burden. This is the "cleaner" measure of financial leverage.

Which ratio would a banker prefer if they only care about Interest-Bearing obligations?

Pillar 2: Coverage

Solvency asks "How much debt?" Coverage asks "Can we afford the monthly bill?"

TIE =
EBITInterest Expense (Gross)

The Logic: Compares Operating Profit (before financing costs) to the mandatory Interest Bill.

Why Gross Interest?

We do not subtract Interest Income. The bank doesn't care if you earned interest on your cash; they care if your business operations generate enough to pay them.

Verizon Example

2021 TIE: 9.31x
2022 TIE: 8.43x

Takeaway

Verizon's cushion shrank. Operating profit covers the interest bill 8.4 times. This is still safe, but the trend is negative (either profit fell or rates rose).

Other Coverage Metrics

Earnings are accrual based. Let's get closer to the cash.

1. EBITDA Coverage

EBIT + D&AInterest

Proxy for Cash. Adds back non-cash Depreciation.

Verizon: 13.17x

2. CFO to Total Debt

Cash from OpsTotal Debt

Actual Cash. Percent of debt we could pay in 1 year.

Verizon: 21%

3. FCF to Total Debt

CFO - CAPEXTotal Debt

Reality Check. After maintaining the factory/network (CAPEX).

Verizon: 8%

Notice the drop? 13x earnings coverage looks invincible. 8% FCF coverage looks tight. This is why we check all three.

The Cash Flow Drain

Can they make the payments? Let's follow the money.

EBITDA (Earnings) - 13.1x Coverage $48 Billion

Huge earnings! EBITDA ignores depreciation (non-cash). Looks great.

Operating Cash Flow $37 Billion

Still strong. We paid the bank and the government.

Why Ratings Matter

Cost of Debt

Lower ratings = Higher interest rates.

Aaa might yield 3.6%, while Baa yields 4.8%. That difference costs millions.

Access

Many institutional investors (like Pension funds) are legally banned from buying "Junk" debt.

The "Cliff": The gap between BBB- and BB+ is the most dangerous place in finance. Falling off means losing half your investors instantly.

Inside the Z-Score

Edward Altman's formula predicts bankruptcy using 5 inputs.

The Formula

Z = 1.2A + 1.4B + 3.3C + 0.6D + 1.0E

Inputs

  • A: Working Capital / Assets (Liquidity)
  • B: Retained Earnings / Assets (Age/Stability)
  • C: EBIT / Assets (Profitability)
  • D: Market Equity / Liabilities (Leverage)
  • E: Sales / Assets (Asset Turnover)

Interpretation Zones

  • Z > 2.99: "Safe" Zone
  • 1.81 - 2.99: "Gray" Zone
  • Z < 1.81: "Distress" Zone

Best Practice

Treat Z-Score as a "Warning Light", not a verdict. Use it to rank firms and spot deterioration trends.

Check Your Understanding: If a company has a Z-Score of 1.5, what zone are they in?

The Z-Score Glitch

Let's feed Verizon's numbers into the model.

CALCULATING...
LIQUIDITY: LOW (Negative Working Capital)
LEVERAGE: HIGH
Z-SCORE: 1.20
PREDICTION: BANKRUPTCY IMMINENT

Wait... they aren't bankrupt?

Verizon 1.2

Verizon has scored in the "Distress Zone" (< 1.8) for 10 years straight.

The Lesson: Models are tools, not laws. The Z-Score penalizes negative working capital (Input A). But for a utility like Verizon, negative working capital is a strategy, not a weakness. False Positive.

Module Summary

Key Takeaways

  • Context is King: A ratio of 3.0x might be great for a Utility but terrible for a Tech startup.
  • Cash Truth: Profits (EBITDA) can be manipulated. Cash Flow (CFO/FCF) is harder to fake.
  • Structure Matters: LGD (Collateral/Covenants) is just as important as PD (Default Probability).
